I've been thinking about education lately. Partly because of all the talk about AI and kids not learning anything anymore, but also because it reminded me of something from my own school days that I never quite figured out.

I love teaching. I've said this before, I'd honestly be quite happy being a teacher or a professor. Back in school I used to teach my classmates during lunch breaks, sometimes people a year or two above me as well. And for a while I got to teach the younger kids, which is where this whole thought started.

## A bit of backstory

My school was a fairly new private school and they let us try stuff. On Teacher's Day and a few other occasions, the older students would take classes for the younger ones. So around seventh grade, I was teaching fifth and sixth graders basic maths, science, social studies and English. Later on I also helped teachers check exam papers, towards the end of middle school and the start of high school.

I really enjoyed it, but there was something weird about teaching the younger classes. They seemed to have a much harder time understanding things than my classmates did. And yes, obviously they were younger. I wasn't expecting them to know the same material as us. I mean things I thought they should have been able to do *at their own grade level*.

The way I remember it, maybe one or two kids in a class of fifty could work through a problem, while in my own class it felt like nine out of ten could do the work meant for us. Those aren't numbers from a study, they're what I remember from around 2017. I don't have the papers anymore or remember the exact questions. Maybe I was explaining things badly, maybe there was something about those classes I didn't know. I was a kid too.

But I remember being confused by the difference. It felt like I couldn't get an idea across, even before we got to actually solving the problem. English comprehension was similar. English was a second language for most of us, but in our school we learned to read and write it before Hindi, so that alone didn't seem to explain much.

I can't tell you what was going on there. It just stuck with me, and then I started noticing other things.

## Grades

My sister is five years younger than me and went to the same school. By the time she got to middle school, it felt quite different from when I was there.

Grades seemed easier to get. I hardly heard about anyone failing. Competition seemed to be discouraged. But what bothered me most was the way the teachers spoke at parents' meetings. I wasn't hearing the sort of criticism I'd heard about my own work a few years earlier.

My teachers had been pretty blunt with us. They talked to us like equals, which I liked a lot, and if our work wasn't good they'd say so. I remember that fondly. With my sister, it felt like everyone had become much more careful about saying anything negative.

I'm not putting her marks here, they're her business. I'm talking about the adults. These were some of the same teachers, at the same school, only a few years apart. I don't know whether they'd been told to change how they spoke to parents, nobody showed me a policy or anything. But I wondered whether they still felt they *could* be honest.

And like, it was a private school. Parents were paying. If your kid comes home with good grades, you're probably happy with the school. If they come home with bad grades, maybe you ask what your kid needs help with, or maybe you ask what the fuck you're paying the school for.

I can see how that puts a teacher in a difficult position. Even if they want to be honest, are they going to get any support when a parent complains? Or will they have to deal with that too?

It reminds me of George Carlin's bit about children and the honour roll. I find it funny, but I also wonder how a parent is supposed to know whether a good report card means their child is actually doing well.

There's a [study of North Carolina public schools](https://fordhaminstitute.org/sites/default/files/20180919-grade-inflation-high-schools-2005-2016_0.pdf) that looked at this. Among students taking Algebra 1 for the first time between 2014 and 2016, 36% of those getting a B didn't meet the state's standard for proficiency on the end-of-course exam.

Now, a course grade covers more than one exam, and you can have a bad exam day. But if I saw a B on a report card, I'd assume the kid understood the subject reasonably well. Wouldn't you? That's a pretty big gap between what the grade seems to say and what the exam found.

## Is it AI?

So I went looking beyond what I remembered from school. Turns out there are reasons to be concerned, though they don't prove my explanation for what happened in those classes.

The OECD's [PISA 2022 report](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en/full-report.html), which looks at how fifteen-year-olds do in reading, maths and science, says the decline in reading and science started before the pandemic. Average reading performance across OECD countries peaked around 2012. The [2025 results](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/09/pisa-2025-students-reading-and-mathematics-performance-declined-sharply-across-the-oecd.html) put reading and maths at their lowest OECD averages so far.

That doesn't mean an entire generation is less intelligent. These tests measure particular skills. But students doing worse at those skills is still something we should care about.

And the dates matter. People weren't using ChatGPT to do their homework in 2012. Whatever AI is doing to learning now, it arrived well after this started. I worry about being able to hand in an assignment you don't understand at all, but blaming AI for the whole problem leaves a lot unexplained.

It isn't only kids either. In the US [adult skills survey](https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/piaac/2023/national_results.asp), the share of adults scoring at the lowest literacy levels went from 19% in 2017 to 28% in 2023. The test format and scoring changed, so the agency warns against treating that as a perfectly clean comparison. It also doesn't tell us how much, if anything, AI had to do with it.

I'm not claiming all these people went through what I saw in my school. I don't know why the scores fell. What bothers me is that there were problems worth looking at long before we had this particular thing to blame.

## Competition

I do miss some of the competition we had in school. I liked knowing how I was doing compared to the others, and having something to work towards. I also liked being able to ask someone who understood a subject better than me.

But before this turns into a rant about how kids need to be put under more pressure, no, I don't want that either. I've seen people my age lose their hair preparing for entrance exams. The JEE and NEET stuff is already far too much imo.

What I want is for a teacher to be able to tell a kid they're struggling and help them with it. Being bad at a subject shouldn't be some huge statement about who you are. You don't understand it yet, okay, let's work on that. Why does it have to become either pretending everything's fine or making the kid feel like shit?

There is some research here too. [Kluger and DeNisi reviewed studies on feedback](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) and found that it helped on average, but in more than a third of the cases it actually made performance worse. They suggest that feedback becomes less useful when it makes people focus on themselves rather than the task they're doing. These weren't all studies of schoolchildren, so you can't turn that into a rule for every classroom.

It does make sense to me, though. My teachers could be direct about my work and still treat me with respect. That's the bit I liked, and I don't see why we should have to give it up.

Rankings get a bit more difficult. I liked them, but if you're always at the bottom and nobody helps you get any better, I can see how you'd eventually stop trying. Just telling someone they're last again doesn't give them much to work with.

I think the useful part is what happens after the grade. Does the kid know what they got wrong? Can they ask for help? Do they get another chance to try it? That's the part I'd want more attention on.

## Phones

I want phones out of schools. I'd also like social media kept away from under-eighteens, though idk how you'd make that work properly.

My school banned electronic devices. I got my first phone at fifteen or sixteen, and before that I'd use my father's if I needed to call someone. We had one CRT television at home and weren't allowed to spend much time on it.

I learned to program from magazines and offline classes on a Sony Vaio that didn't have internet. I didn't get proper internet access until around sixteen. I still loved computers, obviously. I just wasn't online all the time.

I suspect that helped me learn to sit with something difficult for a while. But that's my own experience, and I can't separate it from my family, my teachers or just being interested in the things I was learning. I also can't assume the younger kids had the same home life because we went to the same school. I didn't have a phone in 2017; they might have been using a parent's.

The studies on phone bans don't give me a simple answer either. An [English study by Beland and Murphy](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/dp1350.pdf) found better test scores after bans, especially among students who had been doing worse. A newer [US working paper on schools using lockable phone pouches](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132) found much less phone use, but almost no change in average test scores. Results differed between middle and high schools, and discipline and wellbeing got worse initially before improving later.

Then there's a [separate Florida working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34388) that found better scores in the second year and fewer unexcused absences. Suspensions went up at first, especially among Black students, before that effect eased.

So I still want restrictions, but I can't point to a study and say that taking phones away fixes school. And if scores don't improve after a ban, that doesn't tell us phones were harmless either. It tells us that particular ban didn't improve those scores.

The argument about needing to reach your parents doesn't convince me that you need to be able to use your phone whenever you want in school. There should be a reliable way to contact them, and exceptions for kids who need a device for other reasons. But I don't think wanting to call your parents explains needing access to everything else throughout the day.

## Parents

This is where I start getting annoyed, then remember I don't actually have kids.

I've read accounts from American teachers about parents getting angry with them for saying their child isn't doing well. I don't know how common that is from reading posts online, but I can imagine how frustrating it must be to try to help someone and have their parent argue with you before you've even got to the problem.

At the same time, it's probably hard to hear. You love your kid, you've spent years raising them, and now someone's telling you something you don't want to hear. Maybe they're right. Maybe the teacher is being unfair. You've got to work that out too.

I think parents should be able to accept criticism of their child's work, but I'd also like people to get more help with parenting in general. It's easy for me to sit here and have opinions about it. Ask me how to properly raise or discipline a child and I wouldn't have a complete answer either.

Then there's all the stuff outside school. If both parents are working long hours, who has time to help with homework? Is there family nearby? Somewhere the kid can go and spend time with other kids? If you take away a screen and the alternative is sitting alone at home, you haven't really given them much.

I've gone from teaching fifth graders to working hours and parenting, which is kinda why this is hard to write about. There's always something else that matters. I don't think that means we should stop talking about it, though.

I don't have a solution to education. I just think a kid should be able to find out they're struggling while someone can still help them. If the grades look fine, the parents think things are fine, and the teacher doesn't feel able to say otherwise, how does that happen?

That's what I keep coming back to when I think about my school. I loved teaching those kids. I didn't understand why it felt so much harder with them, and I still don't. But I'd like us to be able to ask about it without immediately blaming the kids, the parents, the teachers, or whatever app is popular this year.

~ A.
